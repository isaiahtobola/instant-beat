DETAIL

Why each body part gets its job

The assignments aren't random. Each channel goes to the body part whose natural motion already matches the part's role in a beat.

Channel 1 · Eyes or Voice

Blink — or Say It → Clap

Two ways to fire the same clap. A webcam watches your eyes and every deliberate blink triggers it — or you talk into a mic and just say “clap.”

Claps live on the backbeat — beats 2 and 4 — sparse and accented, and both triggers match that: occasional, sharp, deliberate. The voice option is Terrence's contribution: for the takes where your eyes are busy watching the screen, your mouth calls the clap instead.

Channel 2 · Finger

One Press → Hi-Hat Roll

One finger, one press on the piano. Beforehand you set the step — a hat every 1, 2, 4, 8, or 16 steps — and that single press writes the whole roll into the grid at that spacing: a blueprint, set once. From there the beat just plays the blueprint — the hats roll step by step in the grid while the finger does nothing. Press again and you set a new blueprint.

Hats are the fastest, densest layer of a beat, and producers burn minutes drawing them into a piano roll one note at a time. Here the spacing is pre-set and the performance is a single press: the finger doesn't strike each hat, it lays down the plan — once.

Channel 3 · Foot

Pedal → Kick / Bass

An electric drum kick pedal plugs straight in. Every stomp is a kick drum — or pitched as the bass line itself.

Real drummers have always played the kick with a foot, because the kick is the heartbeat and feet are metronomes. It is also the one channel that needs zero learning curve: if you can tap your foot to a song, you can play this.

Channel 4 · Two Keyboard Rows

Top Rows → Melody

The newest channel gets its own piano roll, separate from the hi-hat diagram: the number row plays the low octave, and the high row runs Tab to backslash — Tab adds the B tucked under the C, the backslash reaches the top C, a full octave with nothing missing — and PageUp throws the whole roll up an octave when you need more sky. 26 notes under your fingertips, no extra hardware.

B, F and Space were already spoken for by clap, hats and kick, so the tune had to live somewhere untouched. The two rows you never use mid-beat became the instrument: nine sounds — guitar, bass, synths, bells and arpeggios — one Change Sample button away.

THE ASK

Somebody build this into a real plugin

Everything above is a working toy — proof the idea plays. What it wants to be is a real plugin inside a real DAW. Here's the case, plain:

Why it wins · Speed

The piano roll is the bottleneck

Most of making a beat isn't music — it's drawing. Hundreds of mouse clicks to place hats one note at a time, an hour gone before anything grooves.

The Instant Beat replaces drawing with playing. A full beat lands in one four-bar pass, and what's left of the work is the part producers actually love: the performing.

Why it wins · Access

Beats for bodies the mouse locked out

Two of the four channels need no hands at all: eyes or voice fire the clap, a foot plays the kick. The finger's whole job is one press.

For beatmakers with limited hand mobility — or anyone whose hands are busy holding an instrument — this isn't a shortcut. It's the door.

Why it wins · Live

It's a performance, not a file

A beat built this way can be watched. On stage or on stream, an audience sees it played into existence in one pass — no menus, no mouse, nothing to hide behind.

Producers are performers now. This is the first drum workflow designed like it.

Why it's buildable · Today

Small plugin, any DAW

Under the hood it's a MIDI instrument: webcam blink detection, a mic listening for one word, a pedal input — all writing quantized MIDI into the host.

One VST3 / AU plugin — JUCE is the obvious road — drops into FL Studio, Ableton, Logic, any of them. No DAW has to change a thing. The demo on this page is the spec; the sounds above are already synthesized from nothing but math.
